## User constraints and reference

The game remains an original adult first date in a bowling alley on New Year's Eve. Keep the full venue and its activities, two real players, identifiable computer-controlled friends, and the existing simulation and consent rules. The 1970s material palette below is our proposed venue identity, not a user-selected period setting. Every visual—including characters, scenery, materials, signs, particles, and interface artwork—must be authored in code. Procedural geometry, inline vector paths, code-drawn `CanvasTexture` materials, and authored shaders are permitted. Do not import image, texture, sprite, or model assets, use generated images as runtime assets, or embed imported data in source. Browser text can use system fonts. Screenshots document the running code; they are not runtime art.

Use the reference to study the feeling of an intimate, grounded narrative space, not to copy its characters, locations, story, or assets. DON’T NOD's [Life is Strange 2 page](https://dont-nod.com/en/games/life-is-strange2/) describes hand-painted textures and includes an original concept-to-game comparison. In Square Enix's [“Designing the Cast and World of LiS2” interview with Rachele Doimo](https://store.steampowered.com/news/posts/?appgroupname=Life+is+Strange+2&appids=532210&enddate=1574786063&feed=steam_community_announcements), the art director discusses why props belong in a place, building visual character from biography, and managing the cost of lights and geometry.

## Proposed implementation direction

These are engineering and art choices derived from the reference, not claims of user approval or achieved fidelity:

- **Space and camera:** use a human-scale, painterly perspective 3D lounge, with a close-follow, collision-aware exploration camera. Stage a readable two-shot when appropriate for conversation; use activity framing that keeps the actual ball, pins, flippers, pool table, claw, and targets legible. A close frame need not reveal the whole venue at once.
- **Original cast:** author distinct adult faces and body mesh topology, hair shapes, clothing silhouettes, folds, and seams in code. Build grounded idle, walk, turn, and reaction animation with weight and foot contact. A reskinned capsule mannequin is not a character proof.
- **Place and light:** construct real walls, floor, furnishings, and clear walking routes at shared physical coordinates. Use walnut, oxblood, cream plaster, honey maple, aged brass, and restrained green felt. Warm local interior lights, softer shadow transitions, and cooler outer light should establish depth while keeping faces and activity targets readable.
- **Material and story:** code-author painterly material variation, restrained wear, and objects with a reason to be there: shoe storage, seating, ball return, and New Year's preparations. Use contact shadows and physical occlusion; do not punch artificial holes through furniture to expose people.
- **Authority and presentation:** synchronize visible physical objects with server positions and rotations, preserving real outcomes and pool pointer mapping. The clock, text, controls, names, and responsive HUD remain live application elements. Camera and art must not change collision geometry or imply unimplemented actions.

The older Three.js renderer shows that the project can use a perspective camera, lights, materials, and shadows; its rejected primitive room and characters are not a production design to revive. Code-only painting and modeling are technically possible, but expressive faces, hair, fabric, animation, and a coherent scene require substantial original work. **Fidelity to the reference has not been demonstrated.**

## First production proof and gates

The current implementation lives in `src/render/CinematicVenueRenderer.ts` and `src/render/cinematic/`. It includes an enclosed room, four original code-authored characters, continuous skinned limb geometry, head tracking and blinking, batched static geometry, camera collision with the enclosure and selected opaque furniture, a nearby-pair view, and physical activity views. Pool picking uses the live perspective camera rather than the old 2D projection. The latest pass replaces intersecting waist construction, fits the shirt and jacket opening to the torso, shapes the shoe soles, and derives walk/run stride from actual movement speed. The close view now frames the pair from an angle based on their positions, with a slight angle adjustment to reduce background face overlap. Cooler fill, softer shadows, quieter walnut paneling, and code-painted window reflections reduce the room's visual dominance. Faces, clothing, acting, prop scale, and the overall visual richness still need substantial refinement. Technical integration is not visual acceptance.

The [current paired capture](docs/art/cinematic-playtest-current.png) and [close view](docs/art/cinematic-conversation-current.png) record the running code at 1280×800. They are unaccepted development frames, not the target look or a finished scene.

The four characters now use continuous facial geometry with moving eyes, lids and lips. Their hair follows the scalp into the sides and nape. A player’s quick reaction briefly animates their character on both screens. The heart draws one hand inward, “Nice!” adds a nod, and laughter tips the head back. At gameplay distance the two players can see these gestures. Hairlines, jaw and neck contours, some garment edges and the limited range of facial expressions still read as mannequin-like in the close view. The next character review should judge a short exchange in motion, including changes in posture before and after the players act. The room also needs a coordinated scale correction: the pool playing surface is 5.4 by 2.84 simulation units beside characters about 1.78 units tall, and other activity props are similarly oversized. Resizing only their meshes would break agreement with collisions and aiming; revise the shared geometry and physical tuning together, then verify each activity. More decorative props will not resolve these proportion problems.

Build one small, playable section of the actual lounge with two original adults, authored materials and lighting, grounded idle/turn/walk, a close follow view, and a conversation two-shot. Capture real movement and the real HUD at **1280×800 and 1000×700**, including interaction with a physical object and a check that walls or furniture do not hide the pair. The user judges whether this visual proof meets the intended direction before its methods are extended across the venue.

After that proof, complete the connected bowling, pool, arcade, claw, lounge, and arrival spaces rather than treating the slice as the finished game. Verify both clients can move and use each activity with authoritative physics and readable responsive controls. A passing build, an appealing still image, or a conceptual resemblance alone does not pass the visual or gameplay gates.
